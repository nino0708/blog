## Kenzo Tange's Final Tower, Rising above the Dome

At 1-3-61 Koraku, Bunkyo City — in the heart of Tokyo Dome City, just west of the Suidobashi Station — the Tokyo Dome Hotel was completed in 2000, rising to 43 stories and 155 meters. The architect was Kenzo Tange, one of the defining figures in twentieth-century Japanese architecture, responsible for the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building in Shinjuku and the Yoyogi National Gymnasium, among many others. The Dome Hotel stands among his last major commissions before his death in 2005.

The building's white exterior is slender and vertical, its form composed with the clean confidence of an architect who had spent decades shaping skylines. Nearby, the curved translucent roof of Tokyo Dome itself — a giant white pillow of pressurized air — and the tracks of a roller coaster define the ground-level experience. The hotel's slim tower above completes the picture: a vertical anchor for a complex that has always balanced the monumental with the playful.

## An Amusement District That Grew Upward

Tokyo Dome City sits on land with deep recreational roots. The former Korakuen Stadium (predecessor to the Dome) and Korakuen Amusement Park were institutions for generations of Tokyo residents. When the Dome Hotel was added to this mix in 2000, the complex acquired an overnight function that had previously been absent — a hotel that places guests literally above a baseball stadium, within a few hundred meters of a Ferris wheel.

The full 1,006-room count of the hotel is itself a deliberate touch: the number 1006 echoes the Japanese reading of the word "dome" (ドーム), an architectural in-joke embedded in the program. Tange worked with the kind of civic and programmatic scale he had favored his whole career, even at the end, even for a leisure complex: the geometry is unambiguous, the height declarative, the presence unmistakable against Bunkyo's otherwise mid-rise skyline.

## Summary

Tokyo Dome Hotel was completed in 2000, a 43-story, 155-meter tower designed by Kenzo Tange for Tokyo Dome Corporation. It is one of Tange's final large-scale works, and it brings his trademark sense of vertical authority to an unlikely context — a dome, an amusement park, and a garden in the backstreets of Bunkyo. If you visit Korakuen or catch a game at the Dome, look up: the white tower framing the sky above Koraku is part of the same long story that includes the gymnasium at Yoyogi and the twin towers of Shinjuku.
