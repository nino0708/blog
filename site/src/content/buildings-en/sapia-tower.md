## The First Building of Tokyo Station City

Sapia Tower is a 35-story, 4-basement mixed-use tower standing approximately 166 meters tall, completed in March 2007 in Chiyoda City's Marunouchi district. It stands directly connected to JR Tokyo Station's Nihombashi Exit, with a gross floor area of approximately 81,700 m². Both the development and the design were handled by East Japan Railway Company (JR East) — an unusual arrangement that shaped the building's identity from the start.

The name Sapia derives from the English word "sapience" — wisdom or knowledge. This reflects the guiding vision of Tokyo Station City, JR East's ambitious programme to remake the station precinct into a hub of intellect, culture, and industry. During planning it was simply called the JR East Tokyo Station Nihombashi Exit Building.

## A Railway Company's Experiment in Mixed-Use Stacking

Sapia Tower layers its functions vertically: office space through the mid-section, dedicated conference facilities above, and Hotel Metropolitan Marunouchi occupying the topmost floors. The arrangement places business, meetings, and overnight stays within steps of one of Tokyo's busiest rail interchanges — an early demonstration of what transit-anchored mixed-use development could look like in central Tokyo.

JR East's direct role as both developer and designer gave the project an internal coherence unusual for buildings of this scale. The building was conceived not as a standalone commercial tower but as an extension of the station itself. At completion, the office floors were reported to be fully leased, a testament to the unrivalled appeal of a direct Nihombashi Exit address.

## Summary

Sapia Tower, completed in 2007 as a 35-story tower approximately 166 meters tall with a gross floor area of around 81,700 m², was the opening move in JR East's Tokyo Station City project. By stacking offices, conference facilities, and a hotel in a single building with a direct station connection, it showed how a railway company could shape a mixed-use destination from the inside out. From here, it is only a few hundred metres to the red-brick Marunouchi Station building — a walk that holds nearly a century of Tokyo's layered reinvention in a single glance.
