## The Dream of Entering Shinjuku

Takashimaya Times Square opened on October 4, 1996, directly connected to the new south exit of Shinjuku Station. The complex centres on the Times Square Building — 14 floors above ground with four basement levels, reaching a maximum height of 82 metres — alongside a seven-storey South Building. Designed by Nikken Sekkei and constructed by a joint venture led by Obayashi Corporation, the development covers a combined floor area of 174,476 square metres on a site that once held the Shinjuku Freight Station of the Japanese National Railways.

## Freight Yards to Fashion Floors

The redevelopment story begins in 1984, when Shinjuku Freight Station ceased operations. The land passed to Rail City East Development, a special-purpose company established by the Japan National Railways Settlement Corporation, which partnered with Takashimaya and Tokyu Hands as building principals. For Takashimaya, opening here fulfilled an ambition more than thirty years in the making: the department store had been shut out of Shinjuku — Tokyo's busiest commercial district — since the station building was completed in 1964. The name "Times Square," borrowed from the famous New York City square, signalled the building's aspiration to be an international urban entertainment destination. One geographical curiosity: despite carrying "Shinjuku" in its popular name, the building sits just inside Shibuya ward at the boundary with Shinjuku, connected to the station by a pedestrian bridge. Today the South Building houses Kinokuniya Books, a Nitori furniture store, and a performance theatre.

## Summary

Takashimaya Times Square was completed in 1996 with the main Times Square Building standing 82 metres across 14 floors. It occupies the former Shinjuku Freight Yard, transforming a decommissioned rail site into one of Tokyo's largest retail and cultural complexes. Directly accessible from Shinjuku Station's south exit, the building's vast atrium and multi-level retail floors remain a reminder that the most significant urban spaces are often built on the foundations — literal or figurative — of the systems they replaced.
