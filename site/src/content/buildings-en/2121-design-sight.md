## A Folded Steel Roof Over the Garden

On the garden side of Tokyo Midtown, two folded steel plates lie nearly flush with the sloping lawn, their surfaces catching light at different angles throughout the day. This is 21_21 DESIGN SIGHT, a design museum that opened in March 2007. The building has two floors above ground and a total floor area of 1,700 square metres, figures that sound modest beside the towers that surround the Midtown complex — but the numbers are deceptive. Most of the building is sunk into the earth, and what rises above grade is essentially the roof, a 54-metre sheet of steel folded in the manner of origami. The design is by Tadao Ando, whose practice is associated with exposed concrete and rigorous geometry. Here Ando worked within strict height regulations governing the Midtown site, treating the underground as the principal space and the surface as a sculptural presence. The two roof plates settle into the garden as a horizontal artwork rather than a building in the conventional sense, standing in deliberate contrast to the high-rises that loom behind them.

## A Place to Observe Design

21_21 DESIGN SIGHT was conceived as a space for observing and researching design — not simply exhibiting finished objects but examining the relationship between design and everyday life. Fashion designer Issey Miyake was closely involved in developing the concept, and the resulting programme brings in a different director for each exhibition, drawing architects, photographers, graphic designers and artists from across disciplines. The underground gallery is a plain concrete box, but the way light is introduced and controlled transforms its atmosphere entirely from one show to the next. The tucked-away position in the garden, reached only after walking through the greenery of Midtown's grounds, is itself part of the design logic: the act of finding the entrance is a small exercise in attention.

## Summary

21_21 DESIGN SIGHT was completed in 2007, with two floors above ground, a total floor area of 1,700 square metres, and Tadao Ando as architect. Its defining gesture is to sink nearly the entire building underground, leaving two folded steel roof plates as the only visible presence. The museum sits at the meeting point of Roppongi's international energy and the quieter logic of a garden — a place that asks visitors to slow down and look more carefully before they even step inside.
