## A Titanium Spiral Piercing the Mito Sky

Art Tower Mito stands in the Gokkenmachi district of central Mito, Ibaraki Prefecture, a short walk from Keisei Department Store and approximately fifteen minutes on foot from Mito Station. Its defining feature is an approximately 100-meter-tall (99.4736 m) tower built from 57 equilateral-triangle titanium panels arranged in a rising spiral. The complex was designed by Arata Isozaki and opened on March 22, 1990, as the centerpiece of Mito City's centenary celebrations. The tower's geometric form has been compared to Constantin Brâncuși's *Endless Column* and to the double helix of DNA — an open spiral that seems to grow taller as you walk around it.

The tower functions primarily as a landmark: it gives Mito a vertical reference point visible from across the city and has become the most recognizable built image associated with the prefecture. Isozaki, who received the Pritzker Architecture Prize in 2019, counts Art Tower Mito among his principal works in Japan.

## Concert Hall, Theater, and Gallery

The arts program of the complex is housed in a low-rise building of four floors above ground and two below. It contains a 620–680-seat concert hall (ATM) equipped with a large pipe organ, a 472–636-seat playhouse (ACM Theater), and nine contemporary art galleries. An open lawn plaza in front of the building completes the civic ensemble.

The complex sits at the intersection of performing arts and the visual arts in a way that was intentional from the outset. Programming across music, theater, and exhibitions has given the facility a reputation extending well beyond Ibaraki, drawing visitors from across the Kanto region and contributing to debate about the role of public arts institutions in Japanese regional cities.

## Summary

Art Tower Mito opened in March 1990 as a 100-meter titanium spiral tower alongside concert, theater, and gallery spaces designed by Arata Isozaki, commissioned to mark Mito City's centenary. It remains one of the most architecturally distinctive public cultural facilities in eastern Japan.
