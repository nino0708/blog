## A Monument to the City

Walk through Nishi-Shinjuku and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government complex stands apart from the commercial towers around it. Building No. 2 rises 34 floors above ground and three below, to a height of approximately 163 metres. Completed in 1991 alongside Building No. 1, it was designed by Kenzo Tange Associates — the same office responsible for the twin-towered First Building — and completes the three-building ensemble that houses Tokyo's municipal government.

Where Building No. 1 commands attention with its twin towers reaching 243 metres and its Gothic-inflected silhouette, Building No. 2 holds a quieter position to the south. Yet both buildings speak the same architectural language: stone-clad surfaces, geometric layering at the crown, and a sense of vertical ambition held in check by civic solemnity. Together with the Metropolitan Assembly Building, they form a civic complex intended by Tange as a "monument to the city."

## Tange's Legacy in Nishi-Shinjuku

The Tokyo Metropolitan Government moved to Nishi-Shinjuku in 1991 from its previous location in Yūrakuchō, Chiyoda. By that time the sub-centre already hosted a generation of skyscrapers — the Keio Plaza Hotel, the Shinjuku Mitsui Building, the Shinjuku Sumitomo Building — but the arrival of the government campus was the decisive act that certified Nishi-Shinjuku as a true urban centre.

Tange drew on Gothic cathedral composition when designing the complex, creating a vertical hierarchy and a bilateral symmetry that give each building a monumental character unusual among commercial towers. Building No. 2 houses multiple metropolitan bureaux and is connected to Building No. 1 by a covered walkway, making the two buildings a single working administration. Tourists tend to gravitate toward Building No. 1 and its free observation deck; Building No. 2, by contrast, is the quiet workhorse of the pair, carrying the daily functions of the capital.

## Summary

Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building No. 2 was completed in 1991, with 34 floors above ground and a height of approximately 163 metres. Designed by Kenzo Tange Associates as part of a three-building civic complex, it anchors the southern end of the government campus in Nishi-Shinjuku. When walking the sub-centre, step back and read all three buildings together — that is when Tange's vision of a civic monument in the city comes fully into focus.
