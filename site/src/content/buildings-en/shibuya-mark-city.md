## Three Railways, One Building

Walk out of Shibuya Station's west exit and the building fills the view ahead. Shibuya Mark City was completed in February 2000 — 23 floors above ground, three below, standing 95.55 meters tall. Nihon Sekkei and Tokyu Design Consultants designed it as a joint venture. The development arose from an unusual circumstance: the land beneath the site was owned by three separate railway companies — Tokyu Corporation, Keio Corporation, and the Teito Rapid Transit Authority (now Tokyo Metro) — and only by assembling their holdings into a single development could a building of this scale be realized. The result is an office wing (West) and a hotel wing (East, carrying the legacy of the former Keio Inn Shibuya) under one address, bridged directly into the station's west concourse by pedestrian decks.

## The West Side Before the Towers

Shibuya Mark City broke ground in 1994, during the difficult economy that followed the collapse of Japan's asset bubble, and persisted through the decade of uncertainty that followed. When it opened in 2000, the large-scale supertall redevelopment that would later define Shibuya was still years away. The building was for a long time the most prominent structure on its side of the station. Then came Shibuya Hikarie, Shibuya Stream, and Shibuya Scramble Square on the east — and the city began to shift. Shibuya Fukuras was built immediately adjacent on Dogenzaka, pushing the west side's renewal further. Through all of it, Mark City's position at the head of the west exit has remained unchanged, its profile a constant in a station that otherwise keeps remounting itself.

## Summary

Shibuya Mark City was completed in 2000, 23 floors tall, at a height of 95.55 meters. Born from the rare alignment of three railway companies' land, it was the west side's defining structure for the first decade of this century, and remains the first thing you see stepping off the platform onto Dogenzaka. The towers of the east side have grown taller and newer; the west still carries its own weight.
