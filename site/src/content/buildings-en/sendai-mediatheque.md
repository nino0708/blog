## Tubes, Floors, and Glass

Sendai Mediatheque sits on Jozenji-dori, the tree-lined boulevard at the heart of Sendai. Opened in January 2001, the building rises seven floors above ground and two below, housing a city library, a media arts center, and gallery spaces within a single all-glass envelope. Toyo Ito & Associates won a design competition with this proposal, which arrived as something genuinely unlike any public building the city had seen.

The defining element is the tube. Thirteen bundles of steel pipes, each of a different diameter, run vertically from the foundations to the roof. The thinnest measures roughly 0.9 metres across; the widest around 9 metres. Together they carry the weight of flat, columnless floor plates — honeycombed slabs thin enough to read as surfaces rather than structure. Because there are no interior walls or columns, every floor can be arranged freely according to the programme it holds, and the arrangement can change over time.

The exterior is almost entirely glass curtain wall, so the tubes and their shadows are visible from the street at night, the lit floors glowing between them. Ito described the concept with three biological analogies — seaweed, skin, and tubes — suggesting a building that breathes and shifts rather than stands still.

The structure proved itself in March 2011, when the Great East Japan Earthquake struck. The building survived without major damage and briefly served as a refuge for residents displaced by the disaster.

## Summary

Sendai Mediatheque opened in 2001, seven floors above ground, designed by Toyo Ito. The tube-structure system — steel pipe bundles carrying flat, open floors — shaped a generation of architectural thinking about how structure and space can be inseparable. If you are walking Jozenji-dori, the glass box lit from within is impossible to miss. For other Sendai landmarks, [SS30](/buildings/sendai-ss30) (143 m, 1989) stands a short walk away.
