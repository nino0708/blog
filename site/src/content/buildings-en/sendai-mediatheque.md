## A Building That Shows Its Structure

The Sendai Mediatheque opened in January 2001 along the zelkova-lined Jozenji-dori boulevard in Sendai, Miyagi Prefecture. Designed by Toyo Ito & Associates, the building spans seven floors above ground and two below, with a total floor area of 21,682 square meters. Its defining element is thirteen "tubes"—bundled steel columns that penetrate each floor from the foundations to the roof. Each tube houses stairs, elevators, and mechanical systems, freeing the floors of the conventional walls and beams that would otherwise subdivide the space.

Clad in glass and steel, the facade is intentionally transparent, letting passers-by on Jozenji-dori glimpse the activities within—a visual expression of the building's program as an open library, art space, and media center.

## A New Kind of Public Building

Sendai Mediatheque combined a public library, an art museum, and a video media center in a single structure where, unusually, the functions are not separated by solid walls. The spatial continuity between floors and programs made it one of the most discussed public buildings in early 21st-century Japan.

When the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake struck, some of the exterior glass panels were damaged, but the tube structure limited the harm. The building reopened the following year, its structural integrity under seismic load becoming part of its legacy alongside its architectural ambition.

## Summary

The Sendai Mediatheque, completed in 2001 with seven stories above ground and a total floor area of 21,682 square meters, remains one of the most cited examples of structural innovation in contemporary Japanese architecture. Toyo Ito's thirteen tubes dissolve the boundary between structure and program in a way that still feels radical more than two decades on. Walk Jozenji-dori on a winter evening and watch the illuminated interior glow through the full-height glass skin.
