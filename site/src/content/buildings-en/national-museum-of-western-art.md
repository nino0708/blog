## Le Corbusier's Only Building in Japan

The National Museum of Western Art (国立西洋美術館, Kokuritsu Seiyō Bijutsukan) opened on 10 June 1959 at 7-7 Ueno Park, Taito, Tokyo. It is the only structure in Japan designed by the Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier, whose Five Points of Architecture reshaped modernist practice worldwide. The main building rises three floors above a single basement level and covers approximately 3,996 square metres of floor area. In 2016 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of a transnational serial nomination recognising seventeen Le Corbusier works across seven countries.

Executive design and site supervision were carried out by three Japanese architects who had trained at Le Corbusier's atelier in Paris: Junzo Sakakura, Kunio Maekawa, and Takamasa Yoshizaka. Their involvement makes the building a direct product of the post-war exchange between European modernism and Japanese architecture — not an import, but a collaboration.

## The Museum That Grows Outward

Le Corbusier's most distinctive idea embedded in the building is the concept he called the "Museum of Unlimited Growth." The main gallery floors are arranged in a gradually expanding spiral, so that as a collection increases, the building can be extended outward from the perimeter without disrupting the existing layout. Extensions added in later decades proved the concept workable: the building's footprint today is larger than at opening.

The design also demonstrates the Five Points in concentrated form. Pilotis lift the main floor above ground level, creating a sheltered semi-outdoor entrance space. A rooftop sculpture garden brings natural light and Rodin bronzes together under open sky. The rough-textured concrete walls modulate daylight across their surface, changing tone and shadow through the day in ways that reward repeated visits. Walking the perimeter is itself an architectural experience — the building was designed to be circled, not just entered.

## Summary

The National Museum of Western Art, opened 1959, is a three-floor concrete building of 3,996 square metres in Ueno Park, Tokyo. Designed by Le Corbusier with Japanese co-architects, it is Japan's sole UNESCO-listed architectural work and a living demonstration of mid-century modernist principles. When visiting Ueno, allow time to walk the building's exterior slowly: the changing light on its concrete surfaces and the arrangement of Rodin sculptures on the approach make the architecture as compelling as any exhibition inside.
