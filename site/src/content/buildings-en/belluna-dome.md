## A Stadium in the Hills

At the southern foot of the Sayama Hills in Tokorozawa, Saitama, Belluna Dome sits at the terminus of the Seibu Ikebukuro Line. The stadium opened in 1979 as Seibu Stadium, becoming the home ground of the Saitama Seibu Lions (then the Seibu Lions). Its woodland setting distinguishes it immediately from most Japanese stadiums: arriving by train, visitors step out into leafy hillside air rather than a city-centre crowd.

In 1996 a plan was announced to enclose the stadium, and by 1999 the work was complete. Kajima Corporation engineered and built a large-scale membrane roof over the existing outdoor grandstands — a world first in retrofitting a dome over a stadium already in use. Rather than creating a fully sealed enclosure, the design left the outer perimeter open, producing what is known in Japan as a "semi-open dome." Rain cannot fall on the field, but the walls at the edges of the seating bowl remain exposed to the outside air.

## The Character of a Half-Open Stadium

The difference between Belluna Dome and a fully enclosed ballpark is felt the moment you step out of your seat. The roof shelters the playing surface and much of the lower stands, yet fresh air circulates from the perimeter. In summer the heat can be noticeable, but there is also the sensation of wind and natural light that sealed domes cannot provide. This middle ground between an outdoor park and an indoor arena gives the venue an experience of its own.

A major renovation in 2021 modernised the facility — new large screens, a premium lounge beneath the backstop stands, and other enhancements were added. The stadium holds approximately 31,552 spectators. In March 2022 the naming rights were sold and the venue became Belluna Dome. During the Lions season, special trains run on the Seibu Ikebukuro Line to handle matchday crowds, and the station plaza comes alive with fans in blue and silver.

## Summary

Belluna Dome opened in 1979 and was domed in 1999, with a seating capacity of approximately 31,552. The world-first retro-fitted membrane roof created a semi-open arena unlike any other in Japan, and that distinctive character has endured through subsequent renovations. Nestled among the Sayama Hills, the stadium is a destination worth visiting regardless of whether the Lions are at home — the natural setting alone sets it apart from Tokyo's concrete sports venues.
