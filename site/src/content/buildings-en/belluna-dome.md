## A Roof Added to an Existing Ground

Tucked into the wooded hills of Tokorozawa in Saitama Prefecture, Belluna Dome occupies a site that has hosted professional baseball since 1979. The original ground opened as an open-air stadium and became the home of the Lions franchise when the team relocated from Fukuoka that year. The dome roof came later: construction proceeded in two stages through 1997 and 1998, with Kajima Design responsible for the engineering, and the completed dome structure was in place by 1999. The resulting building — with a total floor area of 42,542 square metres — is unusual among Japanese domes in that the retrofit approach left deliberate openings in the structure. Rather than sealing the envelope entirely, the design allows air to pass through the sides, creating an environment that sits somewhere between an outdoor ground and a fully enclosed arena.

## An Open-Sided Dome in the Hills

The practical consequences of Belluna Dome's open structure are felt most keenly in the summer and winter months. Heat dissipates more readily than in a sealed dome on warm evenings, while cold air flows in freely during late-season night games, requiring warm clothing even at the height of autumn. The roof itself is fabricated from PTFE-coated glass-fibre membrane, a material that transmits diffuse natural light during the day, giving afternoon games a softer, outdoor quality unlike the artificial brightness of fully enclosed domes. The stadium has operated under several names over the decades — Seibu Lions Stadium, Seibu Dome, Goodwill Dome, Met Life Dome among them — before becoming Belluna Dome in 2022. The western hills location, served by the Seibu railway lines, places it at the edge of the Tokyo commuter zone, surrounded by green space that marks the boundary between suburbs and countryside.

## Summary

Belluna Dome was completed in its current dome form in 1999, with Kajima Design as architect and a total floor area of 42,542 square metres. The building's defining characteristic is its open-sided structure — a dome that admits natural air rather than sealing it out — the result of adding a roof to a ground that was never designed to be enclosed. The hills of Tokorozawa make the journey itself part of the experience, and the stadium's particular blend of outdoor exposure and covered shelter is unlike anything else in Japanese professional baseball.
