## Twin Towers at the Gate of Osaki

Step out of Osaki Station's east exit and two towers frame the view, facing each other across an open courtyard. Gate City Osaki opened in February 1999, a complex of two 24-story towers reaching 108.8 meters (East Tower) and 98 meters (West Tower), both extending four levels below ground. Nikken Sekkei designed both buildings, combining octagonal, square, and cross-shaped floor plans to reduce shadows, wind pressure, and visual bulk on the surrounding neighborhood — a constraint set by aviation height limits and an intention to make the towers genuinely livable for the district around them. Between the towers, a generous atrium plaza functions as a public passage, softening the scale of what is otherwise a large, complex redevelopment.

## Fifteen Years in the Making

The story of Gate City Osaki begins in 1984, when a preparatory union was established for the redevelopment of what was then an industrial and warehouse district in Shinagawa. The bubble economy's collapse forced repeated revisions to the plan over the following decade. Through those setbacks, the project continued, and when the buildings finally opened in 1999, they had cost an estimated ¥160 billion in total. The development marked a turning point. In the years that followed, the area around Osaki Station became one of the most concentrated office districts in metropolitan Tokyo — anchored by ThinkPark Tower and other business buildings that filled in around the twin towers. The addition of Shonan-Shinjuku Line and Saikyo Line services to Osaki Station amplified the district's appeal, connecting it directly to both Shinjuku and the southern arc of the Yamanote Line.

## Summary

Gate City Osaki was completed in 1999, with 24 floors above ground and a peak height of 108.8 meters. Designed by Nikken Sekkei, the twin towers spent fifteen years in planning before opening as the anchor of a new Osaki. Walking through the courtyard between them, past the carefully calibrated angles of the facades, it is possible to sense the ambition — and the patience — it took to build a new city district from a stretch of warehouses and factories.
