## Stainless Steel Rising over Mejiro-dai

Walk up the slope from Edogawabashi Station on the Tokyo Metro Yurakucho Line, through the quiet residential streets of Mejiro-dai, and a silver silhouette appears without warning. St. Mary's Cathedral, Tokyo — a Catholic cathedral completed in 1964 to the design of Kenzo Tange — announces itself through geometry rather than ornament. Eight hyperbolic paraboloid (HP) shell panels of reinforced concrete rise and converge, clad entirely in stainless steel, narrowing to a cruciform opening at the apex 39.4 metres above. The whole structure reads as a single movement, a folded form that presses skyward the way a Gothic spire does, only in the vocabulary of mid-century concrete.

The cathedral was built on the site of an earlier Catholic church that had stood in Sekiguchi, Bunkyo since the late nineteenth century. The original building was destroyed in the air raids of May 1945, and the congregation worshipped in temporary facilities until a full reconstruction could be funded. Support from the West German Catholic relief organisation Misereor made the new project possible in the late 1950s, and Tange was commissioned to design a cathedral of international standing. The result was completed in the same year as his National Yoyogi Stadium — 1964, the year Tokyo hosted the Olympics — and the two buildings together mark the apex of Tange's postwar output.

## Geometry as Devotion

The HP shell is a ruled surface: a doubly-curved form generated by straight lines, which means it can be constructed in reinforced concrete without the elaborate formwork that free-form curves demand. Tange used eight such shells, their edges meeting in a pattern that traces a cross when seen from above, funnelling natural light down into the nave through the gap at the crown. The total floor area of 3,650 square metres is contained within what is effectively a single above-ground space — the building has one floor above ground and one below — and the interior volume feels entirely in proportion with the exterior drama. The bell tower, standing separately at 61.6 metres, exceeds the cathedral's own height and reinforces the vertical statement of the whole complex.

The cathedral remains in active use for masses, weddings, and other liturgical events. Its architecture has aged without strain: the stainless steel weathers to a muted sheen rather than rust, and the structural logic of the shells is as legible today as in 1964.

## Summary

St. Mary's Cathedral was completed in 1964 in Sekiguchi, Bunkyo — one floor above ground, one below, with the main hall reaching 39.4 metres and the bell tower standing at 61.6 metres. Kenzo Tange's HP shell design, clad in stainless steel, remains one of the most architecturally rigorous religious buildings in Japan and a landmark of postwar modernism. Pair it with the nearby Chinzan-so garden or the Basho Museum in Sekiguchi for a Bunkyo walk that moves between nature and architectural ambition.
