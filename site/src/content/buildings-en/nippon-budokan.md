## Built for the Olympics, Shaped by Tradition

Nippon Budokan (日本武道館) was completed on 15 September 1964 and opened on 3 October of that year — the day after the Tokyo Olympics began — at 2-3 Kitanomaru Park, Chiyoda, Tokyo. Designed by architect Mamoru Yamada and constructed by Takenaka Corporation, it served as the judo competition venue during the Games, the first time judo had appeared as an Olympic sport. The building reaches 42 metres at the roof apex, with an octagonal plan topped by a large suspension roof. At the crown, a gilded finial modelled on a giboshi (擬宝珠, a traditional Japanese architectural ornament) has become the building's most recognisable feature.

Yamada's design sought to express the spirit of Japanese martial arts in structural form. The octagonal geometry echoes forms found in Buddhist temple plans, and the sweeping roof — spanning the arena floor without interior columns — creates an unobstructed space that can shift from a martial arts tournament to a large public assembly at short notice. Seating capacity after later renovations stands at 14,471.

## From Judo to the Beatles and Beyond

Within two years of opening, Budokan hosted an event that would define its second identity. In June 1966, The Beatles performed five concerts here, the first time a rock band had played a venue built for martial arts. The decision was controversial in Japan at the time — traditionalists objected to pop music in a venue dedicated to budo — but the concerts sold out instantly and set a precedent that has held ever since.

Over the decades, playing Budokan has come to signify a milestone in a Japanese artist's career. The phrase "Budokan de yarou" (武道館でやろう, "let's do Budokan") carries the weight of a professional aspiration. The hall's acoustic profile — intimate relative to its capacity, with natural reverb from the curved ceiling — has made it a favourite among performers, and recordings made here ("Live at Budokan" is a common album subtitle) are among the best-selling concert recordings in Japanese music history.

## Summary

Nippon Budokan, completed 1964 at a height of 42 metres, has spent sixty years as the pivot point between martial tradition and popular culture in Japan. Its octagonal dome rises above Kitanomaru Park with a calm authority, flanked by Edo-period gates and the moats of the Imperial Palace. Whether you arrive for a concert, a budo tournament, or simply a walk through the park, the gilded roof ornament and the sweep of the suspension roof make this one of the most recognisable silhouettes in Tokyo.
