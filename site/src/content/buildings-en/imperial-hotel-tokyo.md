## A Century of Hospitality at Hibiya

At the corner where Hibiya Park meets the broad avenue of Uchisaiwaicho, the Imperial Hotel has stood for well over a century in one form or another. The building that greets visitors today — the current main building completed in 1970 — is the third generation on this site. Its predecessors include the first Imperial Hotel of 1890, and the celebrated Frank Lloyd Wright-designed second building of 1923, whose unique floating foundation reportedly withstood the Great Kanto Earthquake largely intact. That Wright building was demolished in 1968 to make way for the present structure.

The 1970 main building reflects the restrained formality of its era, with horizontal stone banding and a low-rise massing that acknowledges the green backdrop of Hibiya Park rather than competing with it. A tower wing followed in 1983, expanding capacity and giving the complex its present silhouette. The hotel has hosted heads of state, royalty, and cultural figures from around the world, consolidating its role as one of Japan's premier venues for high-level diplomacy and ceremony.

## Rebuilding Plans Deferred

Like many large-scale postwar structures in Tokyo, the Imperial Hotel has been weighing a comprehensive rebuild as it approaches its sixth decade. Plans for a new building were under development for several years, but in May 2026 the hotel announced that the timeline had been revised to "undetermined" due to rising construction costs and labor shortages. The current building's future thus remains open — it continues in active operation while the question of its successor is unresolved.

The surrounding Hibiya and Uchisaiwaicho district is one of central Tokyo's most layered neighborhoods: government ministry buildings, the historic Hibiya Public Hall, postwar office towers, and the newer Hibiya Midtown all within a short walk of the hotel's entrance.

## Summary

The Imperial Hotel Tokyo's current main building was completed in 1970, carrying a lineage that stretches back 135 years and includes one of architecture's most famous earthquake stories. While its rebuilding timeline remains uncertain, the hotel continues to operate as a working monument to Japanese hospitality tradition. A stroll along the Hibiya Park side lets you trace the geometry of the building's facade at a distance — worth the detour before exploring the park itself.

Hibiya Midtown and the nearby theater district make Hibiya a rewarding half-day destination for those interested in the intersection of architecture, urban history, and culture.
