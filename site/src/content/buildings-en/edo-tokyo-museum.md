## A Castle Above the Rooftops

A few minutes north of Ryogoku Station, the silhouette of the Edo-Tokyo Museum begins to fill the skyline. The building, completed in 1992, rises seven floors above ground and one below, reaching 62 metres — a commanding presence above the low-rise streets of Sumida. Its most distinctive feature is the raised-floor structure: the entire exhibition volume sits on slender concrete stilts, lifted roughly five storeys off the ground. Visitors reach the main entrance via a long escalator or a bridge-like ramp, arriving at a threshold that feels less like entering a building than boarding a sky-borne vessel. The space beneath the raised floor is left open as a plaza, a sheltered gathering point that animates the site on rainy days.

## Metabolism in Concrete

The museum was designed by Kiyonori Kikutake, one of the architects who founded Japan's postwar Metabolism movement — an architectural philosophy that treated buildings as living organisms capable of growth and change. The massive core structure and the high-raised floor express that philosophy at an urban scale. Inside, the permanent exhibition unfolds across a vast atrium: a full-size replica of the original Nihonbashi wooden bridge spans the floor, and visitors can walk across it. Reconstructed Edo townhouses, merchant shops and kabuki theatre sets are reproduced in meticulous detail, creating an immersive encounter with the city as it existed before industrialisation. The museum opened to the public in 1993 and operated continuously until closing for a major renovation in April 2022; it reopened in March 2026 with updated facilities and displays.

## Summary

The Edo-Tokyo Museum was completed in 1992 and opened in 1993 — seven floors above ground, one below, 62 metres tall. Kiyonori Kikutake's raised-floor structure remains one of the most architecturally distinctive public buildings in Tokyo, its metabolism logic visible from several streets away. Inside, full-scale reconstructions carry visitors through five centuries of the city's layered history, from Edo-period street life to the postwar decades. After its 2026 renovation, the museum stands renewed as a repository of Tokyo's memory. It makes for a natural companion to a walk through Ryogoku's sumo halls and old shitamachi lanes.
