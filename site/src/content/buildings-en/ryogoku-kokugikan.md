## The Home of Sumo: Ryogoku, January 1985

Ryogoku Kokugikan stands in Yokoami, Sumida City, a short walk from JR Sobu Line "Ryogoku Station." Its dedication ceremony was held on 9 January 1985, and the arena has served as the principal venue for professional sumo in Japan ever since. The Japan Sumo Association developed the facility, and the January, May, and September Grand Sumo Tournaments (Honbasho) are held here each year.

The building offers a total floor area of 35,700 square meters and accommodates 11,098 spectators in a mix of traditional box seats (masu-seki) and western-style chairs. The domed roof, in a patinated green-grey finish that evokes aged copper, crowns a wide-span steel structure providing a column-free interior that keeps the dohyo (clay ring) and its surrounding arena in unobstructed view from every seat. The steep rake of the stands is deliberate: maintaining consistent sightlines from the front rows to the uppermost tier was a core requirement of the arena's spatial design.

The Kokugikan's lineage extends to 1909, when the first National Sumo Arena opened at Eko-in temple in Ryogoku. Successive venues operated until the postwar era, after which the neighbourhood's association with sumo was maintained through tournaments held at Kuramae Kokugikan (1954–1983). The present building, sited on the former Kokugikan grounds adjacent to Kokugikan Station, re-established Ryogoku as sumo's permanent home.

## Ryogoku: Where Edo Culture Persists

The streets around Kokugikan preserve a texture rare in central Tokyo. Chanko-nabe restaurants serving the protein-dense stew favoured by sumo wrestlers line the approach roads; a cluster of sumo stables (heya) in which wrestlers train and live occupies the surrounding blocks. The Edo-Tokyo Museum (currently closed for renovation) stands immediately east, and the former Yasuda Garden lies a short walk away.

[Tokyo Skytree](/buildings/tokyo-skytree/), visible from the arena approach, rises just over two kilometres to the northeast — the neighbourhood's new vertical marker set against the low-rise roofline of old Ryogoku. Across the Sumida River and further south, [Tokyo Gate Bridge](/buildings/tokyo-gate-bridge/) connects the reclaimed lands of Tokyo's harbour. The contrast between sumo's centuries-old ritual and the city's relentless infrastructure expansion is nowhere sharper than in this corner of Sumida.

## Summary

Completed 9 January 1985, total floor area 35,700 square meters, capacity 11,098. Ryogoku Kokugikan was developed by the Japan Sumo Association as the permanent home of Grand Sumo in Tokyo, and its distinctive green-grey dome has become inseparable from the identity of the Ryogoku district. Few venues anywhere in the world carry a sport's history so completely within their walls.
