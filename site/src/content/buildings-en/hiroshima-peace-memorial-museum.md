## Walking Through the Pilotis Toward the Dome

Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum stands in Naka Ward, Hiroshima, within Peace Memorial Park, at 1-2 Nakajima-cho. Construction began in November 1953 and the building was completed in March 1955; it opened to the public on 24 August 1955. The design was produced by Kenzo Tange and a group of collaborators including Takashi Asada, Sachio Otani, and Tokunoku Kimura, whose proposal had won first place out of 145 entries in a 1949 design competition.

The building is raised on pilotis — columns that lift the floor slab above ground level, leaving the ground plane open for pedestrians to pass underneath freely. This was a move both structural and symbolic: placing the building on the central north-south axis of the park means that walking northward through the pilotis brings the A-Bomb Dome precisely into view on the horizon. That framed sightline is the building's most deliberate spatial gesture.

## Architecture as Memorial

Tange reinterpreted the raised floor of traditional Japanese architecture in the vocabulary of Modernism, using the pilotis to give the building a "sacred" quality by lifting it off the earth. The long, low horizontal form of the building contrasts quietly with the sky and the trees of the park behind it. Rather than asserting itself, the building creates a stage for the landscape and the history it holds.

The building's architectural merit was formally recognized in 2006, when it was designated a National Important Cultural Property — one of relatively few post-war Modernist works to receive that status. For visitors who know Tange's other work, particularly [Yoyogi National Gymnasium](/buildings/yoyogi-national-gymnasium/) in Tokyo, the Peace Memorial Museum shows an earlier and perhaps more restrained side of the architect's thinking.

## Summary

Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum was completed in March 1955, designed by Kenzo Tange and his group. The piloti-raised building is positioned on the central axis of Peace Memorial Park so that the A-Bomb Dome appears in the frame of the walkway below — a spatial experience that cannot be replicated by photograph. Designated a National Important Cultural Property in 2006, it remains essential viewing for anyone interested in both modern Japanese history and architecture.
