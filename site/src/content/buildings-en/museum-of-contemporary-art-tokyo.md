## A Museum Hidden in the Park

The Museum of Contemporary Art Tokyo sits within Kiba Park in Koto Ward, opening on 28 March 1995. Designed by architect Takahiko Yanagisawa, the building spans three floors above ground and three below, with a total floor area of 33,515 square metres. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government established it as a dedicated contemporary art institution, housing one of the largest collections of post-war and contemporary art in Japan.

Kiba Park was developed on land formerly used as a timber storage and distribution yard — one of Tokyo's great pre-modern industries that gave the area its name (Kiba means "timber yard"). The park's flat, open layout gave Yanagisawa room to design a building that spreads horizontally rather than rising upward, keeping the structure from overwhelming the surrounding greenery. Much of the museum's volume sits below grade, while the above-ground section presents stone-clad facades and geometric forms along the park's axis.

## Architecture and the Collection

Inside, a generous atrium anchors the circulation, drawing natural light deep into the building and connecting the various gallery levels visually. The permanent collection spans post-war Japanese and international art — Gutai group works, pop art, large-scale installations and video pieces among them — while rotating temporary exhibitions occupy a substantial additional footprint. The building underwent a major renovation in recent years, refreshing facilities and exhibition infrastructure while preserving Yanagisawa's spatial composition.

The nearest station is Kiyosumi-Shirakawa on the Hanzomon and Toei Shinjuku lines, a short walk through the park. The surrounding Kiyosumi-Shirakawa neighbourhood has developed into one of Tokyo's most active gallery and design districts, making a combined tour of the museum and the surrounding streets a rewarding half-day itinerary.

## Summary

The Museum of Contemporary Art Tokyo opened in 1995 with 33,515 m² of floor space across six floors, designed by Takahiko Yanagisawa. Its horizontal form nestled in Kiba Park offers a quiet contrast to Tokyo's vertical impulses. Pair a visit with a walk through Kiyosumi-Shirakawa's gallery circuit and discover how eastern Tokyo has quietly become the city's contemporary art heartland.
