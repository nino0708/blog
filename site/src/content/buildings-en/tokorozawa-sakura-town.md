## Stones Piled Against the Musashino Sky

In a district of warehouses and low-rise industry near Higashi-Tokorozawa Station, a building that looks like a mass of stacked granite has risen from the Musashino plateau. The Kadokawa Culture Museum — the centerpiece of Tokorozawa Sakura Town — was designed by Kengo Kuma and Associates in collaboration with KAJIMA DESIGN. The stonework, inspired by the layered geology of the Musashino upland, houses what its creators describe as a fusion of library, museum, and art space under one roof: different modes of cultural consumption pressed together in an unconventional sequence of rooms and galleries. The broader complex opened on November 6, 2020, with a total floor area of approximately 87,433 square meters across its various facilities.

## KADOKAWA's Bet on a Creative City

The project is a joint venture between KADOKAWA — the Japanese media conglomerate that encompasses publishing, anime, games, and digital content — and the city of Tokorozawa. KADOKAWA relocated operational facilities, including warehousing and manufacturing linked to its content production, to the site, and structured the development so that these working functions and public cultural spaces coexist. The intention was a place where the entire supply chain of cultural production, from creation to storage to exhibition, happens in one district — and where visitors can encounter it firsthand. A hotel, shops, and a shrine reconstructed on the property round out the complex. The night illumination of the stone facade has become a draw for visitors from beyond Saitama. In a metropolitan area where most cultural investment clusters around the Yamanote Line, Tokorozawa Sakura Town offers a counterpoint: a significant creative anchor in the outer ring.

## Summary

Tokorozawa Sakura Town opened in 2020, with a total floor area of approximately 87,000 square meters. Built through a partnership between KADOKAWA and Tokorozawa City, and anchored by Kengo Kuma's stone museum, it represents an attempt to build a creative industrial district in the suburban periphery rather than in the city center. Take the Seibu Ikebukuro Line to Higashi-Tokorozawa and walk its paths — the stones have something to say about what Japanese culture is making of its next chapter.
