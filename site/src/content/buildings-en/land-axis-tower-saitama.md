## A Landmark in a Planned New City

At Shintoshin 11-2, Chuo-ku, Saitama — a short walk from Saitama-Shintoshin Station, served by the JR Keihin-Tohoku and Takasaki lines — stands the Meiji Yasuda Life Saitama Shintoshin Building. Its common name is LAND AXIS TOWER. Completed in March 2002, the building rises 35 storeys above ground (plus two mechanical levels and three basement floors) to a height of 168.3 metres. Meiji Yasuda Life Insurance was the developer, and Nikken Sekkei handled the design.

Saitama Shintoshin is a planned urban district built on the enormous former Omiya freight-yard site. Redevelopment began in earnest in the mid-1990s with the aim of concentrating national government offices and business functions in a location outside central Tokyo. LAND AXIS TOWER occupies the symbolic centre of that scheme: its 168-metre profile defines the skyline of a city that was, not long before, flat rail infrastructure.

## A Government-Led New Town

One distinctive feature of Saitama Shintoshin's formation is the role of national relocation policy. Ministries and agencies — including regional offices of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and a Self-Defence Force facility — were moved here to reduce functional concentration in central Tokyo. That public-sector anchor helped pull in private-sector tenants, and LAND AXIS TOWER was developed within that current of demand.

Nikken Sekkei's design for the tower is direct: a clean rectangular volume that aligns with the grid of the new district rather than competing with it. The word "axis" in the building's nickname points to exactly this: the tower serves as a reference point along the central spine of the planned city, readable from the station forecourt and the wide streets of the new district.

## Summary

The Meiji Yasuda Life Saitama Shintoshin Building (LAND AXIS TOWER) was completed in March 2002, rising 35 storeys to 168.3 metres on the former Omiya rail-yard site. Developed by Meiji Yasuda Life Insurance and designed by Nikken Sekkei, it is the visual centrepiece of a planned new city built on a policy of decentralising Tokyo's office functions.

Step off the train at Saitama-Shintoshin and walk toward the tower: the combination of wide planned streets, government facilities, and a 168-metre axis makes the city-building ambitions of the 1990s legible in the concrete and steel in front of you.
