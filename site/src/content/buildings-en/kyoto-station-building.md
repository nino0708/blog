## A Gateway That Became a Monument

Step off a Shinkansen at Kyoto and look back before you leave the platform end: the Kyoto Station Building fills the sky. Not with height — at 60 m and 16 floors above ground it is modest by Tokyo standards — but with sheer horizontal scale. The facade runs roughly 470 metres from east to west, enclosing a total floor area of 235,942 m² across a hotel, department store, shopping mall, cinema complex, and multi-level car park. It is, among other things, the largest station building in Japan by floor area, and its signature space — the Grand Staircase under a soaring glass atrium — has become one of the most photographed interiors in the country.

## Controversy, Competition, and a Daring Choice

The building's origin lies in a 1990 international design competition. The brief asked for a new gateway befitting Japan's ancient imperial capital, which drew entries ranging from the reverently low-key to the unapologetically futurist. Hiroshi Hara's entry, developed with his practice Atelier Phi (formally Atelier Phy Architectural Research Office), was selected. It was, notably, the entry with the lowest proposed height — a detail that did little to quiet the debate about whether such a bold geometric structure belonged at the door of Nijo Castle and the Heian-period streetgrid.

Completed in 1997 after the previous Meiji-era station was demolished, the building uses a steel frame behind a glass-and-steel curtain wall to create a series of internal canyons lit from above. The Grand Staircase connects the ground-floor concourse to a rooftop sky promenade, drawing visitors upward through a building that rewards exploration. Hiroshi Hara, better known for his theoretical writings and the Umeda Sky Building in Osaka, produced here his most publicly visited work.

## Summary

Kyoto Station Building, opened in 1997, is the work of architect Hiroshi Hara and the product of a contentious but ultimately brave design choice. Its 16 floors, 60 m height, and nearly 236,000 m² of space make it a city unto itself — hotel, shops, cinema, and Shinkansen concourse all under one glass roof. Alongside the [Kyoto Tower](/buildings/kyoto-tower/) across the street, it frames an entrance to the ancient city that is entirely, unmistakably modern.
