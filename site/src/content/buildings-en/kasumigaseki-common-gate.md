## Twin Towers in Japan's Administrative Heart

Kasumigaseki is the nerve centre of Japan's national government, home to the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and dozens of other central agencies. In September 2007 a new landmark rose in this historically low-rise district: Kasumigaseki Common Gate. The complex comprises two towers — the West Tower, at 38 floors above ground, three below, roughly 176 m tall and 119,000 m² of floor space, and the East Tower alongside it. A sky-bridge at the third floor links the pair, and a direct underground passage connects to Exit B2 of Kasumigaseki Station, making the building one of the most convenient office destinations in central Tokyo.

## Renewing a District Frozen in Time

For much of the late twentieth century the area around Kasumigaseki Station changed little. Office buildings from the 1960s and 70s clustered alongside the ministries, and the district projected an image of bureaucratic permanence. By the early 2000s, however, those aging structures had become candidates for renewal, and Common Gate was one of the first large-scale projects to bring Grade-A office space to this address.

The tower offers column-free floor plates and generous ceiling heights suited to contemporary workplace layouts. The lower floors host retail and dining, creating a hint of public life in a quarter that otherwise runs on government schedules. The glass-and-stone curtain wall holds a conversation with the 1968 [Kasumigaseki Building](/buildings/kasumigaseki-building/), Japan's first skyscraper, which still stands nearby — two eras of Japanese office architecture facing each other across the same block.

## Summary

Kasumigaseki Common Gate, completed in 2007, anchors the redevelopment of Tokyo's central government district. The West Tower's 38 floors and 176 m height make it a visible landmark in a neighbourhood defined more by institution than by skyline ambition. For visitors who want to experience the quiet intensity of Japan's administrative core, a walk through Kasumigaseki — past the old building and the new — is a window into how the country governs itself.
