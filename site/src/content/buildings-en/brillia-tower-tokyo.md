## An Eastern Landmark in 2006

At 4-1-1 Taihei, Sumida Ward, a 45-story residential tower was completed in June 2006. Brillia Tower Tokyo stands 159 metres tall and was delivered by Tokyo Tatemono, the developer behind the Brillia brand of residential condominium towers. Located a short walk north of Kinshicho Station — where the JR Sobu Line meets the Tokyo Metro Hanzomon Line — it brought high-rise residential density to an eastern ward of Tokyo at a time when such towers were still associated primarily with the bay-front.

The mid-2000s marked an acceleration of Tokyo's supertall residential boom. While [Deux Tours Kachidoki](/en/buildings/deux-tours-kachidoki/) and [The Tokyo Towers](/en/buildings/the-tokyo-towers/) were preparing to reshape the waterfront skyline to the west, Brillia Tower Tokyo established a comparable scale inland, in Sumida Ward — a district better known for the shitamachi streetscapes around Asakusa and the industrial history of the Sumida River.

## Brillia Brand and the Four-Developer Model

Tokyo Tatemono registered the Brillia brand in 1998 and built it into one of Japan's most recognisable residential condominium brands. Brillia Tower Tokyo — 644 units across 45 floors — remains one of the brand's most substantial early projects and gave it a vertical presence in the eastern half of Tokyo.

The development consortium brought together Tokyo Tatemono, Tobu Real Estate, Keisei Electric Railway, and Nippon Tochi-Tatemono: a cross-section of railway-related and real estate interests typical of large urban residential developments in Japan, where railway operators routinely participate in station-adjacent land development.

## Summary

Brillia Tower Tokyo was completed in June 2006 with 45 floors above ground, two basement levels, a height of 159 metres, and 644 residential units. Developed by Tokyo Tatemono and partners in the Kinshicho area of Sumida Ward, it is a significant early example of the Brillia brand's high-rise ambition and of the spread of supertall residential development into Tokyo's eastern wards.

Standing below the tower in Taihei, you can look south along the Sumida River's curve toward the bay-front towers that followed — a panorama that traces twenty years of Tokyo's residential high-rise history in a single glance.
