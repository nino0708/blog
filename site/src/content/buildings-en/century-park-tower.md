## A Waterfront Pioneer at 180 Metres

Where the Sumida River curves sharply through central Tokyo, the former industrial island of Tsukushima was reinvented from the 1980s onward as one of the city's most ambitious waterfront housing developments. Century Park Tower, completed in March 1999, stands at the heart of that transformation. Rising 54 storeys and 180 metres above Tsukuda 2-chome in Chuo City, it was developed by Mitsui Fudosan, designed by Nihon Sekkei, and built by a joint venture of Mitsui Construction and Taisei Corporation. Its 756 units made it one of the largest residential towers in Tokyo at the time.

The building is part of the River City 21 complex, a large-scale redevelopment of the Ishikawajima-Harima Heavy Industries shipyard. Where heavy machinery once shaped iron hulls, Century Park Tower and its companion residential blocks now offer river views from high above the Sumida. The white façade is clean and unadorned, letting the tower's sheer verticality speak for itself against the waterway below.

## Defining the Waterfront Era

The late 1990s were a formative period for high-rise residential development in Tokyo. Century Park Tower was among the first to demonstrate that families and long-term residents — not just investors — would choose to live in a supertall building on reclaimed bayfront land. The model it established was influential: the Harumi, Kachidoki, and Toyosu waterfront districts followed in subsequent years with their own clusters of residential towers, each owing something to the template that River City 21 and Century Park Tower helped set.

The tower is accessible by subway on the Tokyo Metro Yurakucho Line (Tsukishima Station), and the Sumida River promenade runs directly below. The riverside walk connecting Tsukuda Park to Kachidoki Bridge is one of Tokyo's quieter urban pleasures — a place where the city's industrial and residential pasts overlap in a stretch of water and greenery.

## Summary

Century Park Tower was completed in 1999 — 54 storeys, 180 metres tall, 756 units. Developed by Mitsui Fudosan and designed by Nihon Sekkei, it stands as one of the defining buildings of Tokyo's waterfront residential era. Walk the Sumida River promenade, look up at the tower's silhouette reflected in the water, and imagine the moment this stretch of the city transformed from shipyard to skyline.
