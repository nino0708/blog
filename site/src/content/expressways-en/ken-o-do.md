## A Vast Ring Around Greater Tokyo

The Metropolitan Inter-City Expressway — shortened in everyday speech to Ken-O Expressway (圏央道, Route C4, National Route 468) — traces a sweeping arc through the outer metropolitan area at roughly 40 to 60 kilometres from central Tokyo. Its planned total length is approximately 300 kilometres. The route intercepts the radial expressways that fan out from the capital: the Tomei, Chuo, Kan-etsu, Tohoku, Joban, Higashi-Kanto and the Tokyo Bay Aqua-Line. Along the way it passes through Yokohama, Atsugi, Sagamihara, Hachioji, Ome, Kawagoe, Kuki, Tsukuba, Narita and Kisarazu — a cross-section of the region's suburban and industrial cities.

The first section opened on 26 March 1996, linking Ome IC in Tokyo with Tsurugashima Junction in Saitama. Extensions through Kanagawa, Saitama, Ibaraki and Chiba followed in stages over the next two decades. On 26 February 2017 the full Ibaraki section between Sakai-Koga IC and Tsukuba-Chuo IC opened, completing the link between the Tohoku, Joban and Higashi-Kanto expressways on the eastern side of the ring — all without passing through central Tokyo.

## A Logistics Backbone Across the Metropolitan Fringe

The expressway's defining role is lateral connection: it intercepts the radial motorways and reroutes traffic between them without entering the congested urban core. Without such a ring, a lorry travelling from Yokohama to Utsunomiya would have to traverse central Tokyo's elevated highways. Ken-O turns that cross-city detour into a wide outward sweep. Distribution centres and industrial parks have clustered along the corridor as a result, reshaping the logistics map of the metropolitan fringe.

Ken-O occupies the outermost ring in Tokyo's three-ring structure. The [Gaikan Expressway (C3)](/expressways/tokyo-gaikan-expressway/) forms the middle ring at roughly 15 kilometres from the centre, while the [Shuto C2](/expressways/shutoko-c2-yamate-tunnel/) runs as the innermost loop. Together the three rings convert what was once a purely radial network into a layered grid. Sections in Kanagawa (the Yokohama-Shonan Road) and part of Chiba remain under construction, so the ring is not yet fully closed.

## Summary

Ken-O Expressway opened its first section in March 1996 and has extended in stages over more than two decades, approaching its planned 300-kilometre total. It connects seven radial expressways and the Tokyo Bay Aqua-Line, giving the metropolitan region a freight and commuter bypass that avoids the urban core entirely. The ring's incompletion is a reminder that even the largest infrastructure projects unfold across generations. Drive Ken-O on a clear day and Tokyo's concrete skyline gives way to the rice paddies of northern Saitama, the Tama hill ridgeline, or the Pacific horizon near Kisarazu — the expressway runs through a very different landscape from the city it encircles.
