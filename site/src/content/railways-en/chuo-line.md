## An Orange Spine from the Kobu Railway

The Chuo Line Rapid traces its origins to 11 April 1889, when the Kobu Railway opened between Shinjuku and Tachikawa. That August the line reached Hachioji, sketching early the axis that would connect central Tokyo with the Tama district. Later nationalised as part of the Chuo Main Line, the rapid service between Tokyo and Takao now runs 53.1 kilometres as the familiar "orange train."

The orange-vermilion band on its carriages has been the line's signature since the 101-series trains of 1957. Alongside the yellow of the Sobu local line and the light blue of the Keihin-Tohoku Line, it is part of the colour-coded language by which Tokyo commuters read their network at a glance.

## A Line That Runs Straight West

The Chuo Line's most distinctive trait is its straightness. From Higashi-Nakano to Tachikawa the track barely bends, holding a run of straight line unusually long for an urban railway. That ruler-straight alignment is a legacy of the Meiji era, when the flat Musashino plateau was cut through in a single sweep to lay the tracks.

The service pattern is layered. Beyond the all-stations rapid run the daytime Chuo Special Rapid and Ome Special Rapid, which skip stations to link Tokyo and Shinjuku with Tachikawa, Hachioji, and Takao quickly; morning and evening add commuter specials. Around Yotsuya and Ochanomizu the line runs on a quadruple-track section beside the Sobu local trains, the two services dividing the work between them.

## Summary

Since the Kobu Railway of 1889, the Chuo Line Rapid has connected central Tokyo with the Tama district over 53.1 kilometres. Its orange carriages and the long straight from Higashi-Nakano to Tachikawa are the line's signature scenery. Ride it through from Tokyo to Takao and the view shifts continuously — from the offices of Marunouchi to the towers of Shinjuku, then the Musashino suburbs, and finally the green foothills of Mount Takao.
